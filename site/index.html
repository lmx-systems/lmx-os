The problem

Every distributor runs a delivery operation. None of them wants to.

Industrial last mile is the same job in every sector: urgent, low-density B2B deliveries that keep a repair bay, a job site, or a plant floor from going idle. Auto parts alone accounts for roughly 350 million deliveries a year in the United States, about $6 billion of cost — and almost none of it is charged to the customer receiving the goods.

That delivery is run by the distributors themselves. Their own vehicles, their own drivers, their own dispatcher on their own phone. It is a cost they absorb to protect a sale, not a business they chose to be in.

The economics are upside down

Delivery frequently costs more than the margin on the part being delivered.

The market is fragmented

Rooftops three miles apart, each with its own fleet and its own dispatcher. Nothing is shared.

Nobody commits to a time

Ask a distributor for a delivery guarantee and you will not get one. They cannot make it, so they do not offer it.

Spot solutions do not hold

Gig platforms give a release valve and take away route memory, accountability, and the relationship.

The shops always want the parts 20 minutes ago at the cheapest price possible — but I'll never commit to a delivery time, because I can't.

Why now

Autonomous delivery is arriving. It is not arriving here.

Amazon operates its own drone delivery program across a dozen states. Walmart delivers by drone from more than a hundred supercenters. Serve runs sidewalk robots in five states. Uber expects a substantial share of its delivery volume to be handled by machines.

All of it is consumer. None of it reaches the industrial last mile — the urgent, business-to-business layer that sits between a distributor and the professional who cannot work without the part.

The reason is structural. Autonomy companies supply a spot solution: a vehicle, a route, an app. An industrial distributor needs a full solution — dispatch, exceptions, returns, proof of delivery, a fallback when the machine can't run, and one party accountable for all of it.

That gap is what LMX closes.

The company

One operator. One contract. One delivery promise.

LMX OS

Our own dispatch system. It takes in orders, assigns urgency, decides what to hold and what to send, chooses how each drop travels, and re-plans the whole fleet continuously as conditions change. It learns from every delivery we run.

Fleet

Drivers and vehicles under LMX control and LMX standards. Operational discipline from people who ran delivery networks at Amazon, applied to an industry that has never had it.

Autonomy

We are the operator; autonomy providers are our partners. Their vehicles plug into our network. We keep the delivery promise regardless of which one carries the part.
